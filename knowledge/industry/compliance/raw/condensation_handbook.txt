1 Background  
Condensation occurs when water vapour in the air changes into liquid water. If this 
moisture does not dry as quickly as it forms, it can accumulate and create conditions that 
adversely affect building performance.  
Condensation results from complex interactions between environmental conditions, 
building design and construction, and occupant behaviour. Buildings designed today are 
also expected to perform under future conditions, including more frequent and intense 
extreme weather events and changing climate patterns. These changes can introduce 
combinations of rainfall, humidity, and seasonal temperature extremes that differ from 
those traditionally assumed in building design.  
At the same time, higher levels of insulation and improved airtightness are changing how 
moisture behaves within buildings.
 As less heat moves through the building fabric, the 
drying capacity of materials is reduced. As a result, moisture that enters or accumulates 
within the building fabric may persist for longer periods. 
These circumstances indicate that traditional condensation management strategies based 
only on established expectations, rules of thumb, or narrow margins of safety may no 
longer be sufficient. Accordingly, a more informed and proactive approach is increasingly 
necessary. 
The NCC includes minimum requirements intended to reduce interstitial condensation 
within walls and roof spaces. However, effective condensation management also depends 
on decisions made during design, construction, and occupancy. Designers, builders and 
building occupants all have a role in managing condensation. 
This handbook introduces condensation in buildings, explains its causes and potential 
impacts, and outlines practical options to assist with managing condensation through 
design, construction and occupancy decisions. It supports users in understanding and 
applying the applicable NCC provisions but does not replace the NCC or provide 
compliance determinations.

Alert  
Condensation is an NCC defined term. It means the formation of liquid water either on 
the surface of a building element or material or within the building fabric as a result of 
moist air coming into contact with a surface that is at a lower temperature. 
Surface and i nterstitial condensation  
Condensation within a building may occur as visible surface  condensation, or on 
surfaces within the building fabric, known as interstitial condensation. 
While water ingress is a common cause of moisture-related problems, the focus of this 
handbook is on managing moisture within the building’s fabric that results from 
condensation.

2 Scope 
This handbook helps practitioners understand condensation risks and their potential 
consequences for building performance and occupants’ health. It explains the minimum 
NCC requirements for managing water vapour and condensation and offers practical 
guidance to assist practitioners to understand and apply those requirements. It also 
outlines good practice approaches that may help manage condensation risk, noting that 
these approaches are not mandatory unless required by the NCC. 
While the NCC applies primarily to new buildings, this handbook also provides guidance 
that may assist with managing condensation risks in existing buildings. It should be read in 
conjunction with the NCC provisions relating to damp and weatherproofing, energy 
efficiency and construction in bushfire-prone areas. 
Many of the situations discussed in this handbook relate to homes. Residential buildings 
are often designed and built with limited access to specialist advice on condensation risks 
associated with new materials and construction methods. During renovations, changes 
that affect a building’s ability to manage moisture are not always identified or fully 
assessed.  
Where relevant, commentary is included for larger commercial buildings. However, the 
handbook does not address in detail the wide range of indoor environments, building 
systems and operating conditions typically found in larger or more complex commercial 
buildings.

3 Condensation management in the NCC  
In Australia, condensation in buildings is becoming increasingly common. Modern 
construction methods, together with energy efficiency and fire safety requirements, are 
driving building design toward more airtight and highly insulated building envelopes. 
These measures reduce heat loss, limit fire spread and improve overall building 
performance.  
However, airtight homes without adequate ventilation can trap moisture within the building 
and its fabric. This problem becomes more severe where there is a large temperature 
difference between the inside and outside of the building, particularly in cooler climates or 
in humid, tropical and subtropical regions where air-conditioning is used.  
This chapter explains the intent and key concepts of the NCC condensation management 
requirements. These requirements aim to reduce the likelihood that condensation or water 
vapour will cause illness, or loss of amenity for building occupants.  
The NCC condensation management requirements apply to the following residential 
buildings: 
• Class 1 buildings  
• Sole-occupancy units (SOUs) within Class 2 buildings  
• Class 4 parts of buildings. 
For further information on NCC building classifications, refer to Part A6 of Section A 
Governing Requirements in NCC 2025, Volumes One, Two and Three, or the NCC 
Navigator: Building classifications. 
The condensation management provisions in NCC 2025 build on work undertaken through 
multiple studies and resources, including a scoping study completed in 20161. To support 
industry adoption, the NCC introduced these measures progressively, with the first 
condensation provisions included in NCC 2019 and additional measures introduced in NCC 
2022. 
The condensation management requirements are located in: 
• Part F8 of NCC Volume One 
• Part H4 of NCC Volume Two 
• Part 10.8 of the ABCB Housing Provisions Standard (the Housing Provisions).  
To support these requirements, the NCC adopts a number of referenced documents, 
including Australian Standards and industry standards. These documents are relevant to

F8V1 Condensation management  
(1) Compliance with Performance Requirement F8P1 is verified for a roof or external 
wall assembly when it is determined that a mould index of greater than 3, as 
defined by Section 6 of AIRAH DA07, does not occur on— 
(a)  the interior surface of the water control layer; or 
(b) the surface of building fabric components interior to the water control layer.  
(2) The calculation method for (1) must use— 
(a)  input assumptions in accordance with AIRAH DA07; and  
(b)  the intermediate method for calculating indoor design humidity in Section 
4.3.2 of AIRAH DA07. 
 
Verification Method F8V1 references AIRAH DA07 Criteria for Moisture Control Design 
Analysis in Buildings (2021), published by the Australian Institute of Refrigeration, Air-
Conditioning and Heating (AIRAH). AIRAH DA07 sets out performance-based design 
criteria for predicting, mitigating, or reducing moisture-related damage to the building 
envelope, materials, components, systems, and furnishings. The methods in AIRAH DA07 
may be applied using software packages capable of completing hygrothermal 
calculations.2 Additional information on AIRAH DA07 is provided in Appendix D.1.4 of this 
handbook. 
Verification Method F8V1 requires confirmation that a roof or external wall assembly does 
not exceed a mould index greater than 3 on either: 
• the interior surface of the water control layer, or  
• the surface of building fabric components interior to the water control layer. 
Mould Growth Index (MGI)  
The Mould Growth Index (MGI) is a numerical scale ranging from 0 to 6 that quantifies 
the extent of mould growth on building materials. 
 
A water control layer means a pliable building membrane, or the exterior cladding where 
no pliable building membrane is present. A pliable building membrane is a water barrier as 
classified in accordance with Australian Standard, AS 4200.1 Pliable Building Membranes 
and Underlays – Materials.

The mould index must be calculated using the input assumptions in AIRAH DA07, including 
the intermediate method for calculating indoor design humidity set out in Section 4.3.2. 
A mould index equal to 3 represents visible mould growth on less than 10% of the material 
surface, or up to 50% coverage when viewed under a microscope. Mould index values 
below 3 indicate mould growth that is detectable only microscopically. 
AIRAH DA07 sets out input assumptions to be used with hygrothermal analysis software 
when calculating the mould index. These assumptions include the mould sensitivity class 
of the material, humidity conditions and exposure time.  
The mould sensitivity class describes a material’s propensity to support mould growth. For 
example, glass and metal are classified as low-sensitivity materials and are generally 
resistant to mould growth. In contrast, untreated wood contains nutrients that support 
biological growth and is therefore classified as very sensitive to mould growth.  
To calculate the mould index, the Verification Method also requires the use of input 
assumptions from AIRAH DA07, specifically Section 4.3.2 Indoor design humidity, 
intermediate method. 
Section 4.3.2 of AIRAH DA07 provides a standardised method for determining indoor 
design humidity using hourly weather data and assumptions about the heating, ventilation, 
and air-conditioning (HVAC) system likely to be used. Using this approach helps ensure 
consistent input assumptions and calculation procedures for indoor humidity when 
determining the mould index.  
3.3 DTS Provisions 
The DTS Provisions provide a prescriptive approach for demonstrating compliance with 
the NCC condensation management Performance Requirements. These are Performance 
Requirement F8P1 in NCC Volume One and Performance Requirement H4P7 in NCC 
Volume Two.  
The intent of the DTS provisions is to reduce the risk of moisture becoming trapped within 
the building fabric in ways that may lead to mould growth or material degradation. They do 
this by specifying where materials are required to permit or restrict the movement of water 
vapour and by identifying where ventilation is required to support drying. 
When using a DTS Solution: 
• NCC Volume One:  Compliance with Performance Requirement F8P1 is achieved by 
complying with Clauses F8D2 to F8D6 of Part F8 Condensation management.  
• NCC Volume Two:  Compliance with Performance Requirement H4P7 is achieved by 
complying with H4D9 which references Part 10.8 of the Housing Provisions.

3.3.1  External wall construction  
Clause F8D3 in NCC Volume One and Clause 10.8.1 in the Housing Provisions set out DTS 
requirements for the construction of external walls.  
These clauses manage water vapour by prescribing the use of building membranes or  
sarking-type materials, or by requiring ventilation of the wall cavity, depending on the 
climate zone and wall construction. Together, these measures are intended to reduce the 
risk of moisture accumulation within the wall assembly. 
The following sections explain these requirements in more detail, including control layer 
performance, vapour permeance, and drained and ventilated cavity provisions. 
3.3.1.1  Control layer requirements for external walls  
To manage the movement of water vapour within an external wall, the NCC requires the 
installation of a pliable building membrane, or a sarking-type material to function as a 
control layer. In certain locations, this control layer must also be permeable to water 
vapour. Any pliable membrane or sarking-type material must comply with AS 4200.1 and 
be installed in accordance with AS 4200.2 Pliable Building Membranes and Underlays, 
Part 2: Installation Requirements. 
The NCC requirements reflect differing moisture risks across climates. In cooler climates 
(for example Melbourne and Hobart), higher vapour permeance control layers support 
outward drying of internal moisture. In tropical climates (for example, Cairns and Darwin), 
lower vapour permeance materials help limit inward moisture drive from the exterior. 
Material selection within the wall assembly also influences vapour movement. Open-cell 
insulation products, such as mineral wool or fibreglass, typically have high vapour 
permeance, while closed-cell insulation products, such as polystyrene, generally have low 
vapour permeance.

VCMs are classified into four classes. Class 1 has the lowest vapour permeance (highest

Alert

can diffuse through a material. Vapour permeance is measured in µg/N.s (micrograms 
per newton-second) and is determined in accordance with ASTM4-E96, Procedure B 
Water Method at 23°C and 50% relative humidity (RH).5

3.3.2  Exhaust systems  
The DTS Provisions in NCC Volume One and the Housing Provisions set minimum exhaust 
flow rates for bathrooms, sanitary compartments6, kitchens and laundries.7 The intent of 
these provisions is to ensure that buildings can effectively remove moisture that would 
otherwise increase the risk of condensation. 
Clause F8D4 of NCC Volume One and Clause 10.8.2 of the Housing Provisions require that 
exhaust air from a kitchen, kitchen range hood, bathroom, sanitary compartment, or 
laundry must be discharged to outdoor air, either directly or via a duct or shaft.  
Where a venting clothes dryer is installed, it must also discharge to outdoor air, either 
directly or via a duct or shaft. This requirement does not apply to condensing clothes 
dryers, which capture water vapour during operation and condense it into liquid water for 
disposal. 
In an SOU within a Class 2 apartment building, where no clothes dryer is installed but 
space is provided for a clothes dryer in accordance with subclause F4D2(1)(b)8, provision 
must also be made to allow a clothes dryer to be ducted to the outdoor air.

4 Condensation concepts  
This chapter provides background information to explain how condensation occurs in 
buildings and the factors that influence it. It explains how air and moisture, including liquid 
water and water vapour, move through the building fabric and how materials absorb, 
store, and release moisture.  
This information supports NCC users by improving their understanding of condensation 
risks and the underlying principles that influence condensation management. It is intended 
to assist practitioners in interpreting and applying the NCC condensation provisions, rather 
than prescribing compliance solutions. 
4.1 Why condensation matters 
Condensation is the process by which water vapour in the air changes into liquid water. 
When the moisture formed by condensation does not dry as quickly as it accumulates, it 
can build up inside a building and lead to problematic moisture conditions. 
Condensation within a building may occur as visible surface condensation, or on surfaces 
within the building fabric, known as interstitial condensation.  
Interstitial condensation can be more damaging than surface condensation because it is 
often concealed and may go unnoticed. Where the building fabric is not designed to allow 
moisture to dry from within, moisture can become trapped. Over time, this can 
compromise the durability of the building and adversely affect the health and amenity of 
occupants. 
4.2 Water vapour and occupancy 
Even in buildings that appear dry, moisture exists as both liquid water and water vapour. 
Liquid water is typically retained within plumbing systems such as pipes, drains and 
storage tanks to support building operation. Water vapour, however, is more directly 
relevant to condensation risk. It is always present in the atmosphere, mixes freely with air, 
and moves wherever the air goes. 
What is water vapour?  
Water vapour is the gaseous form of water present in the air. 
 
Air moves continuously inside and around buildings (see Figure 4.1). Ventilation with 
outdoor air is essential for occupant health and comfort and may occur through open

moves unintentionally as it leaks through gaps in the building enclosure in response to 
changing pressure conditions. In addition, outdoor air circulates through roof spaces, wall 
cavities and under-floor spaces. All this air movement carries water vapour with it.  
There is always some water vapour in the atmosphere, even in the world’s driest places. In 
Australia, atmospheric water vapour content can fall to less than 0.25% by mass in alpine 
areas during winter and may reach almost 2% in the tropical north during the summer wet 
season.

4.3 Sources of water vapour in buildings 
In addition to the water vapour present in outdoor air, indoor moisture levels increase due 
to normal occupant activities. People release water vapour through respiration (breathing) 
and perspiration (sweating). Additional water vapour is generated by daily activities such 
as cooking, washing and showering. In a typical dwelling, a family of four can generate 
between 7 kg and 22 kg of water vapour per day, depending on occupancy patterns, daily 
activities and ventilation rates. 
In new construction, the building fabric itself can also be a source of moisture. Concrete 
slabs and steam-cured concrete blocks commonly retain residual moisture from 
manufacturing or curing processes, which is gradually released into the indoor 
environment. Figure 4.2 illustrates common water vapour sources in homes.

4.4 Relative humidity (RH) 
Relative humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air, expressed as a percentage of 
the maximum amount the air can hold at a given temperature. Understanding the factors 
that influence RH is important, as condensation typically occurs at or near 100% RH at the 
surface and prolonged RH levels above approximately 50% can increase the likelihood of 
mould growth. 
Air can exhibit high RH at low temperatures even when it contains only a small amount of 
water vapour. If the water vapour content of the air remains constant and the temperature 
increases, the RH decreases. 
Figure 4.3 illustrates how RH changes as temperature rises when the amount of water 
vapour is fixed. In this example, the same quantity of water vapour represents 100% RH at 
an alpine temperature of -5°C, but corresponds to progressively smaller RH values at 
warmer temperatures.

4.5 Saturation and dew point 
Temperature determines the maximum amount of water molecules that air can hold. When 
this limit is reached, the air is saturated. If the air cools beyond this point, the excess water 
vapour condenses into mist or liquid droplets until a new saturation limit is reached at the 
lower temperature. 
The relationship between temperature and water vapour can be visualised using a 
psychrometric chart. 9  Figure 4.5 shows a simplified chart used for introductory purposes. 
A key feature of the chart is the diagonal curve that separates the gridded vapour zone 
below from the ice and water zones above. The point where a vertical temperature line 
intersects this curve represents the saturation limit for that temperature. Reading 
horizontally from this point to the vertical axis indicates the water vapour content of the air 
at saturation. This curve (also called the dew point line ) represents the dew point 
condition for each temperature.

What is dew point?  
Dew point is the temperature at which the RH of the air reaches 100% saturation, and 
water vapour contained in the air begins to condense. A higher dew point indicates that 
the air contains more water vapour. Conversely, a lower dew point indicates drier air.  
Relative humidity alone does not indicate how much water vapour is present unless 
temperature and pressure are also known.

4.6 Relative humidity, comfort, health and mould risk 
Relative humidity plays a critical role in indoor comfort and health. When RH is too low, the 
air becomes excessively dry, causing irritation of the eyes, skin and respiratory 
membranes, and contributing to dehydration and fatigue. These symptoms are common in 
environments such as passenger aircraft cabins, where RH is often around 10%. For 
comfort, RH of 30%– 60% is commonly suggested, although this range does not relate to 
any specific temperature.

4.7 Material responses to relative humidity 
The response of different building materials to RH is a key consideration in condensation 
management. Many common building materials are hygroscopic, meaning they can absorb 
or adsorb moisture from their surroundings. This behaviour influences how moisture 
moves through the building fabric and affects both the extent and consequences of any 
condensation that forms. 
Hygroscopic materials attract and retain water vapour from surrounding air. Adsorption 
typically occurs first and involves the attachment of water vapour molecules to the 
microscopic surfaces of porous materials without forming liquid water. This interaction 
can significantly influence material performance and durability. 
As hygroscopic materials approach their adsorption capacity, they can also take in liquid 
water through capillary suction (wicking) and absorption, storing moisture within pores 
and cracks. For example, timber can increase its moisture content to around 25–30% at 
about 98% RH by adsorbing water vapour from the air. Timber that becomes saturated by 
liquid water can hold several times more moisture in its pore structure. A material’s ability 
to adsorb and store moisture depends not only on its material properties, but also on the 
vapour pressure and temperature of the surrounding air. 
Hygroscopic materials respond primarily to RH, rather than the absolute amount of water 
vapour in air. They absorb more moisture when RH is high and release it as RH falls. 
Because RH increases as air temperatures fall, cooler conditions tend to promote greater 
water vapour storage within hygroscopic materials. This behaviour can provide a 
temporary hygric buffer, moderating changes in moisture levels as water vapour levels 
increase or temperatures fall. 
However, experience shows that relying on the hygric capacity of the building envelope 
alone is not a reliable strategy for preventing moisture problems. Some of this capacity is 
needed to manage periodic wetting from rain, surface water or rising groundwater. 
Construction moisture within the building fabric itself (such as water retained in concrete 
slabs or in steam-cured concrete blocks) must also be considered.

4.8 Water vapour diffusion through materials 
While most water vapour in buildings is transported by moving air, some also moves by 
diffusion. Water vapour molecules can pass through materials that block liquid water and 
even air. When there is a higher concentration of water vapour on one side of a material 
than on the other, vapour will naturally diffuse through the material towards the lower 
concentration.

4.9 Climate 
When considering condensation, at least two climates need to be taken into account:  
• the outdoor climate , shaped by local weather conditions, and  
• the indoor climate , which may be intentionally controlled through design or may arise 
incidentally from building use.  
Indoor conditions are influenced by occupants’ activities and by how they respond to 
changing outdoor conditions to maintain comfort. The outdoor and indoor environments 
are inherently connected, as buildings must bring in fresh outdoor air and expel stale 
indoor air through ventilation, infiltration and exfiltration.  
The exchange of heat, air and water vapour across the building enclosure – through 
surfaces that appear solid – determines the temperature and moisture conditions within 
interstitial spaces of the building envelope (see Figure 4.9). These interstitial conditions 
play a critical role in condensation risk.

4.9.1  Climate classifications  
Climate classification systems used in building design typically focus on environmental 
factors that affect human comfort. Using this approach, relatively broad groupings can be 
sufficient to set fundamental thermal design strategies. For example, as few as four basic 
climate types are commonly used for this purpose (Szokolay 1995).  
The four basic climate types are as follows. 
• Cold climates : Conditions in which people feel too cold outdoors for most or all of 
the year, due to low temperatures or excessive heat loss. 
• Temperate climates:  Conditions characterised by insufficient heat in the coolest 
season and excess heat in the warmest season, although neither is particularly 
severe. 
• Hot dry climates:  Conditions with high outdoor temperatures and relatively low 
humidity, allowing effective evaporative cooling of the body. Day–night temperature 
variations are typically large, with cooler nights providing relief from daytime heat. 
• Warm humid climates:  Conditions with high temperatures and high humidity, where 
limited evaporation reduces cooling effectiveness. Small diurnal (day-night) 
temperature variations mean warm conditions persist overnight. 
The eight climate zones used by the NCC energy efficiency provisions are subdivisions of 
these four basic climate types. They reflect differences in typical temperatures and the

resulting insulation requirements for buildings. The interactive NCC climate map is

While the NCC climate zones are effective for determining thermal performance, they 
provide only limited indication of condensation risk. 
In addition to the climate zones, the NCC uses a separate climatic classification for DTS

full range of conditions relevant to condensation risk across Australia. 
Because neither the NCC energy efficiency climate zones nor the humidity-based  
subfloor zones were developed specifically to assess condensation, more detailed local 
climate information can provide a more reliable basis for managing condensation risk.  
4.10  Outdoor condensation potential 
In the absence of a single accepted method for assessing condensation risk across all 
climates, Australian building practitioners can draw on local climate information. Among 
the most useful sources are the Bureau of Meteorology (BoM) climate statistics, which 
provide monthly mean minimum and maximum temperatures averaged over the period of 
record for each weather station. These records also include monthly mean dew point 
temperatures for 9am and 3pm, which indicate the atmospheric water vapour content at 
those times.

4.11  Indoor climate and water vapour loads 
Differences in temperature and water vapour content between outdoor and indoor 
environments strongly influence building envelope design and detailing. Because air is 
continually exchanged between indoors and outdoors, indoor water vapour levels 
generally reflect outdoor conditions unless moisture is actively reduced through effective 
dehumidification. While this may occur in some air-conditioned buildings, depending on 
plant capacity and control settings, it is uncommon in Australian homes, particularly during 
the heating season.  
For design purposes during the heating season, it is therefore conservative to assume that 
outdoor water vapour content represents the starting point for indoor humidity conditions. 
Daily average indoor water vapour levels in homes generally depend on several interacting 
factors, including: 
• the amount of outdoor air introduced through ventilation 
• internal sources of water vapour 
• the amount of water vapour that can be vented directly outdoors 
• the rate at which water vapour is removed through ongoing ventilation.

5 Creating dry buildings  
5.1 Overview 
This chapter outlines practical strategies for minimising condensation and supporting the 
delivery of dry buildings. It focuses on how building design and construction decisions 
influence moisture behaviour and condensation outcomes.  
The chapter addresses: 
• the design and performance of building envelopes 
• indoor environmental conditions, including relative humidity 
• condensation within interstitial spaces 
• the role of control layers for water, air, vapour and thermal control 
• approaches to assessing condensation risk, including calculation methods and 
relevant software tools. 
Together, these topics are intended to support practitioners in applying the concepts 
outlined in earlier chapters and in understanding how different design and construction 
choices can influence condensation performance. Unless otherwise stated, the strategies 
discussed are explanatory and should be read in conjunction with the relevant NCC 
provisions.

5.2 Envelopes (claddings, linings and control layers) 
Once a building is enclosed and occupied, its internal environment will differ from the 
external environment. These differences are usually most noticeable during the hottest 
and coldest weather, when heating or cooling is used.  
Differences in temperature, air pressure, and water vapour conditions (including dew 
point) across the building envelope drive the movement of heat, air and moisture. The 
building envelope must manage these flows to limit condensation risk and support indoor 
comfort and durability. 

===== PAGE 43 =====
The online version of this handbook is the official, current document. Printed or saved copies may be out of date. 
  
abcb.gov.au  Condensation in building handbook  Page 42 
 
Effective condensation management relies on appropriate design and construction of the 
building envelope, combined with strategies to control internal moisture sources. 
Adequate ventilation is essential to limit moisture accumulation and reduce the likelihood 
of condensation. 
To manage RH and condensation, four interrelated layers of control are typically involved: 
• Water  control : The first priority is to control liquid water. Rain and groundwater are 
the main causes of surface wetting, and, if poorly managed, can penetrate deeper 
into the building fabric. Wet building elements are more vulnerable to condensation 
and are slower to dry. 
• Air control : Because water vapour and heat are transported with air, the envelope 
should guide air movement along intentional pathways as much as possible. 
Uncontrolled leakage through gaps, cracks and penetrations can undermine efforts to 
manage moisture and heat flow.  
• Water vapour control : Water vapour can also move through permeable materials by 
diffusion. Vapour control does not require completely blocking this movement. In 
many cases, slowing diffusion during one season while allowing drying during 
another can be beneficial. Appropriate strategies depend on climate, building use and 
envelope construction, and must remain consistent with NCC requirements. 
• Heat control : The NCC sets minimum energy efficiency requirements in Section J of 
Volume One and Section H of Volume Two. These provisions specify minimum 
thermal resistance (R-Value) requirements for walls, floors and roofs by climate zone. 
Meeting these requirements helps to control heat flow, support internal surface 
temperatures remaining above the dew point, and support indoor conditions with 
acceptable RH. Where the NCC specifies vapour permeance requirements, meeting 
these requirements supports drying of interstitial spaces, particularly in cold climates. 
In hot, humid climates, air-conditioning can create cooled surfaces that promote 
condensation, highlighting the need for appropriate vapour control strategies.  
Ideally, a single material would provide all four control functions across the entire building 
envelope. In practice, factors such as weather exposure, durability requirements, cost, 
and construction methods mean that multiple materials are usually required. These 
materials together form the control layers within the interstitial spaces between exterior 
claddings and interior linings (see Figure 5.1).

5.3 Changes to interstitial conditions in the building 
envelope  
Changes in environmental conditions, regulatory requirements and construction practices 
have altered the moisture and temperature conditions within buildings. In combination, 
these changes can increase the likelihood of condensation within the interstitial spaces of 
the building envelope.  
Interstitial condensation risk is commonly associated with higher indoor RH and cooler 
surface temperatures that approach the dew point of indoor air. These conditions may 
arise because of increased airtightness, higher levels of insulation, changes in material 
selection, and evolving patterns of building use. 
Effective management of interstitial condensation relies on proportionate design and 
construction measures that both limit moisture ingress and support adequate moisture 
removal and drying within the building envelope.

5.6.1  Roof cladding condensate  
Condensation beneath roof cladding commonly occurs when moist air in the roof space 
comes into contact with the underside of the roof covering.  This risk is particularly 
evident under metal roofing, where night-sky radiative cooling can cause the roof surface 
temperature to fall several degrees below the surrounding air temperature.  
To manage the risk of condensation in roof spaces, the NCC includes requirements for 
roof-space ventilation in Clauses F8D5 and F8D6 of Volume One and Clauses 10.8.3 and 
10.8.4 of the Housing Provisions. These provisions also apply to tiled roofs that

The online version of this handbook is the official, current document. Printed or saved copies may be out of date. 
  
abcb.gov.au  Condensation in building handbook  Page 48 
incorporate a control layer above the primary insulation layer, as this construction can 
reduce natural airflow through the roof covering. These provisions are described in 
Chapter 3 of this handbook. 
In addition to ventilation, condensation risk beneath metal roofing can further be reduced 
by installing a roof blanket or sarking. Sarking can intercept condensate forming on the 
underside of the roof sheeting and direct it toward gutters. In this role, the sarking 
functions as a water control layer, or drainage plane, preventing moisture from dripping 
onto ceiling linings or insulation below. 
While sarking must be waterproof, it does not necessarily need to have a high resistance 
to water vapour. In some situations, the ability of sarking to absorb and temporarily hold 
water can be beneficial – particularly where condensate levels are high and drainage paths 
to gutters are limited. This may occur under low-pitched roofs, where the roof sheet 
profiles or fixings restrict water flow.  
Where sarking is impermeable to water vapour, condensation forming above it is largely 
driven by humid outdoor air circulating through the roof covering and cavities. Figure 5.5 
illustrates how condensation can form on the underside of roof cladding when night-sky 
cooling causes the cladding temperature to fall below the dew point of the external air 
moving through roof corrugations. In these situations, effective drainage is essential to 
avoid moisture accumulation. 
Condensation may also form on the underside of the sarking itself. This risk can be 
reduced by ventilating the roof space to lower humidity levels. Installing sarking below 
battens, rather than directly beneath the roof cladding, can further reduce this risk by 
limiting heat transfer from the roof covering.  
The use of spacers or battens can interrupt thermal bridging between the roof cladding 
and the sarking, raising the sarking temperature and reducing the likelihood of 
condensation. These spacers can also improve drainage and ventilation above the sarking. 
This approach is illustrated in industry guidance such as the Australian Steel Institute’s 
(ASI) Condensation Management for Steel Roofing. The ASI illustration also shows gaps at 
the top of the sarking under the ridge capping to allow ventilation to be maintained under 
the sarking.

5.8 Water vapour control layers 
Where the dew point temperature of the air is substantially higher than the temperature of 
interstitial surfaces, limiting water vapour movement towards colder surfaces can reduce 
the risk of condensation under certain conditions. 
However, Australian research and overseas experience show that this approach does not 
perform well in all climates or seasons. A vapour control layer that reduces condensation

risk at one time of year may increase risk at another by restricting drying or trapping 
moisture within the building fabric. 
In very cold climates, vapour barriers have traditionally been located on the warm side  of 
the building envelope to limit the migration of warm, moisture-laden indoor air into 
progressively colder outer layers.  
In hot, humid tropical climates, a similar approach has been applied by locating vapour 
barriers on the external side of the envelope to limit water vapour movement towards 
cooled interior surfaces in air-conditioned buildings. 
These approaches can work in climates that are consistently cold, or consistently warm 
and humid. In climates with mixed seasonal conditions, however, experience shows that 
vapour barriers often create more problems than they resolve.  
While some locations in Australia experience a predominantly single season climate, most 
are characterised by seasonal variability. Increasing use of air-conditioning further 
complicates vapour flow behaviour by reversing the direction of vapour drive during 
summer, making it difficult to identify a single suitable location for a vapour barrier. 
Building-science research, including work by John Straube (2001) and observations by 
Lstiburek (2011), consistently indicates that: 
In many practical situations, a low-permeance vapour barrier will not improve 
hygrothermal performance and may in fact increase the likelihood of damaging 
condensation or trapping moisture in the system. A common misconception regarding 
low-permeance vapour barriers is that their inclusion where one is not technically 
needed provides an extra level of performance and resistance to moisture problems. 
Quite the opposite is true. 
Lstiburek’s work on moisture management emphasises balancing the need to limit 
moisture ingress with the need to allow assemblies to dry. Key principles, paraphrased 
below (Lstiburek 2011b), include: 
• preferring vapour retarders over vapour barriers where lower resistance is sufficient  
• avoiding vapour retarders where vapour-permeable materials can achieve the 
required performance 
• avoiding vapour barriers on both sides of an assembly, so drying can occur in at least 
one direction 
• recognising that vapour barriers on interior surfaces can increase condensation risk
 
in air-conditioned buildings 
• noting that impermeable interior finishes (such as vinyl wall coverings) can increase 
condensation risk

5.8.2  Water vapour control under floors  
The suspended timber-framed floor illustrated in Figure 5.13 is designed to allow water 
vapour to diffuse readily into the ventilated subfloor space. In this approach, higher indoor 
water vapour levels are intended to be dissipated through subfloor ventilation.  
However, experience in air-conditioned housing in the United States, has shown that this 
strategy can lead to moisture problems under summer conditions. In these cases, a 
reverse (inward) vapour drive can occur, where moist air from the subfloor migrates 
upward towards the cooler interior (Lstiburek 2008).  
As this moisture migrates upward, condensation may form on surfaces with high vapour 
resistance, such as vinyl flooring or the bases of furniture and cupboards in direct contact 
with the floor. Moisture accumulation within fibrous insulation can also result in elevated 
RH, increasing the risk of mould growth and timber decay in floor framing. 
One proposed mitigation strategy is the installation of foil-faced foam insulating board 
beneath the floor joists to act as a vapour control layer (see Figure 5.13). This approach 
relies on achieving a continuous airtight and vapour-tight installation which can be difficult 
to achieve in practice.  
If successfully sealed, the foil-faced insulation layer may also restrict downward drying.  
This restriction can increase the risk of trapping liquid moisture resulting from leaks or 
spills above the floor. These trade-offs highlight the importance of carefully assessing 
local climate conditions, building use and moisture sources before adopting vapour control 
measures under floors.

5.9 Risk assessment calculation methods and software 
Condensation risk within building assemblies can be assessed using a range of calculation 
methods and software tools. These methods are commonly grouped into steady-state and 
transient approaches. 
Both approaches are designed to model moisture behaviour within building elements. 
However, they differ significantly in their underlying assumptions, level of complexity, and 
the physical processes they are able to represent. As a result, they are suited to different 
types of assessment and design stages and levels of detail. 
The following sections outline the key characteristics of each approach and discuss their 
typical applications and limitations when assessing condensation risk. 
5.9.1  Steady -state methods  
Steady-state methods assess condensation risk by modelling the diffusion of water 
vapour through the materials and spaces of a building envelope. This is done by 
comparing the calculated dew point (or water vapour pressure) of the diffusing water 
vapour with the temperature of the surfaces it passes through.  
In these methods, water vapour is assumed to follow a simple, one-dimensional path 
through the assembly. As vapour diffuses through successive layers, its water vapour 
pressure decreases in proportion to the vapour resistance of each material layer. 
Steady-state methods assume indoor and outdoor air temperatures and dew points remain 
constant over the assessment period. In practice, this is typically represented using 
monthly average temperatures and dew points, with results accumulated over the course 
of a typical year.  
Steady-state condensation calculations can be performed manually and are often 
presented graphically, showing temperature and dew point (or partial water vapour 
pressure) profiles through the building assembly (see Figure 5.14).

5.9.2  Transient methods  
Transient condensation risk assessment methods aim to represent the dynamic interaction 
between climate and building materials over time. They seek to reflect the complex reality 
of how heat, liquid water and water vapour move through and are stored within building 
assemblies. 
Unlike steady-state methods – which consider heat conduction and vapour diffusion under 
fixed conditions – transient methods model a wider range of interacting processes over 
time, including: 
• wetting by liquid water 
• storage of heat and moisture within materials 
• temperature effects associated with evaporation and condensation 
• changes in material properties as temperature and humidity (among other factors) 
vary over time. 
Because of this complexity, transient methods rely on computer‑based simulations. These 
models may represent building assemblies in one or two dimensions, depending on the 
level of detail required and the nature of the assessment.

Transient modelling requires extensive input data, including detailed climate information 
and material properties. The reliability of results depends not only on the quality of this 
data, but also on the modeller’s understanding of building physics principles and the 
implications of modelling assumptions and input choices. Australian-specific climate 
databases should be used to ensure relevance. 
Both steady-state and transient methods have limitations. In particular, most modelling 
tools do not explicitly represent bulk air leakage paths or the water vapour transported 
with uncontrolled airflow. These effects are typically addressed through design 
assumptions rather than direct simulation. 
Within these limitations, transient tools continue to be developed and refined based on 
experience from research and practice. When applied appropriately by experienced 
practitioners, they can provide useful insights into: 
• the persistence of adverse combinations of indoor and outdoor conditions  
• the relative performance of alternative construction assemblies 
• the seasonal and long-term moisture behaviour of the building envelope.12 
 
Alert  
Transient condensation assessment methods and associated software require specialist 
expertise and careful interpretation. Meaningful results depend on informed modelling 
choices, appropriate input data and professional judgement. Accordingly, engagement of 
suitably qualified and experienced professionals is recommended.

6 Useful tips for design, construction and 
occupancy of buildings  
Effective management of condensation and moisture is essential to maintaining building 
durability, energy efficiency, and occupant wellbeing throughout a building’s life.  
This chapter provides practical guidance to help designers, builders, and occupants 
minimise condensation risks across the entire building lifecycle: 
• during design , through informed material selection, detailing and performance 
strategies  
• during construction , through appropriate installation, sequencing and quality control. 
 
Reminder  
Condensation risk is affected not only by design and construction, but also by how a 
building is occupied and used. Detailed consideration of occupancy and operational 
factors is outside the scope of this document. 
 
The guidance in this chapter is intended to complement earlier chapters by translating key 
principles into practical considerations. Unless otherwise stated, the information is 
explanatory and should be read alongside the relevant NCC provisions. 
6.1 Design tips 
6.1.1  Climate analysis  
Confirm NCC compliance   
• Confirm that the design meets the NCC minimum requirements. Refer to Chapter 3 of 
this handbook for the application of NCC requirements relating to external walls, roof 
spaces, and exhaust systems. 
Understand the outdoor climate  
• Use reliable, site-specific climate data to understand local temperature and humidity 
conditions. Where necessary, consult suitably qualified professionals to validate 
assumptions. 

===== PAGE 68 =====

Define the indoor climate  
• Consider intended activities, occupancy patterns and likely sources of water vapour. 
Where future use is uncertain or may change, adopt conservative (worst-case) 
assumptions for moisture generation and operating conditions.  
Identify climate separation within the envelope  
• Determine where control layers establish the boundary between indoor and outdoor 
environments within the building envelope. Use this information to determine critical 
surface temperatures and undertake preliminary checks of interstitial condensation 
risk. 
6.1.2  Designing the building envelope  
Vapour resistance and drying potential   
• Confirm that the vapour resistance of control layers within proposed floor, wall, and 
roof assemblies complies with NCC requirements. Identify the layer with the highest 
vapour resistance and ensure that other layers have substantially lower resistances. 
Arrange layers to support drying to the inside, outside, or both as appropriate to the 
climate and assembly type. 
Surface temperature control   
• Design the assemblies so that critical surfaces – such as wall wraps, or sheathing 
located on the exterior side of insulated stud frames – remain above the indoor dew 
point under relevant design conditions. Account for the effect of insulation on both 
sides of these surfaces, including the thermal resistance of reflective surfaces facing 
still air spaces. 
Services and ventilation requirements   
• The following design considerations support NCC compliance and effective moisture 
and condensation management: 
• Consider providing adequate ventilation for water-heating systems installed within 
subfloor spaces. 
• Vent flue gases from boilers and water-heating systems directly to the exterior, 
including systems installed in non-habitable spaces, garages, subfloor spaces, and 
roof spaces. 
• Ensure mechanical exhaust air from bathrooms, laundries, and kitchens is discharged 
beyond the exterior wall or roof in accordance with the NCC. 

===== PAGE 69 =====

• Specify condensing clothes dryers, or externally flued dryers, in accordance with the 
NCC. 
• Ensure air-conditioning ductwork passing through roof spaces is adequately 
insulated and sealed to limit heat loss, air leakage and condensation risk. 
6.1.3  Communicating the condensation control strategy  
Explain the design intent   
• Clearly communicate the condensation and moisture-control strategy to the builder 
and, where appropriate, support its implementation during construction. This should 
include the relevant strategies, materials, performance data and critical detailing 
needed to: 
• minimise the risk of internal surface condensation (including control of thermal 
bridging), and  
• limit the risk of interstitial condensation (including air-leakage control and correct 
installation of membranes or sheet materials that comply with specified limits for 
water vapour transmission resistance). 
Clarify membrane roles and limitations   
• Explain the purpose, required performance and limitations of all membranes and 
sheet materials installed between the internal lining and external cladding. Clearly 
identify which layers may be penetrated for services and which must remain 
continuous and unpenetrated to function as control layers.  
Verify installation   
• Where appropriate, confirm that control layers and materials are installed in 
accordance with the design intent and specifications. Ensure that any design or 
construction changes are reviewed to assess their potential impact on condensation 
risk. 
Document and hand over   
• Record the final condensation control strategy, including any approved changes 
made during construction, and clearly communicate the key features to building 
owners and occupants as part of handover documentation. 
Support  occupant moisture management   
• Where appropriate, provide local source extraction and incorporate background or 
trickle ventilation to help occupants manage moisture levels during normal building 
use.

6.2 Construction tips 
6.2.1  Confirming the design intent before construction  
Identify what matters and why   
• Where not already provided, seek clear advice from the designer on which elements 
of the design are critical to managing condensation risk and why they are important. 
This helps ensure that critical elements are understood and prioritised during 
construction. 
Understand the intended control layers  
• Condensation strategies often rely on separate layers to manage liquid water, air, 
water vapour, and heat within the building envelope. Confirm the intended role and 
performance of each control layer and clarify any uncertainties with the designer 
before work begins. 
Confirm assembly requirements  
• Different condensation strategies may apply to roofs, walls, and floors, and may vary 
within the same element where construction details change. Confirm with the 
designer the exact location, continuity and specification of all relevant control layers 
and materials for each situation.  
Review existing buildings   
• Where alterations or additions are proposed, consider existing construction and 
moisture-management measures, including: 
• identifying any existing strategies used to minimise condensation risk that may be 
retained 
• where documentation is unavailable, consider assessing whether:  
• roof spaces were designed to be ventilated or airtight 
• penetrations through linings were sealed or unsealed 
• membranes or sheet materials were used to control air or water vapour movement in 
walls, roofs, or floors.

6.2.2  Maintaining the design intent during construction  
Communicate with all relevant trades   
• Brief site personnel responsible for installing critical materials on the condensation 
control strategy. Ensure all trades – particularly services installers – understand which 
layers must not be penetrated, altered or substituted. 
Manage construction moisture   
• Sequence wet trades (such as slabs, screeds, plastering, jointing, tiling) to allow 
maximum drying time before enclosure or application of finishes. 
Avoid unapproved material changes   
• Do not substitute, remove, or add materials that affect condensation control without 
designer approval. Seemingly beneficial changes, such as extra insulation or 
waterproofing, can increase condensation risk if they restrict drying or alter dew 
point conditions. Ensure the specified performance requirements for critical materials 
are clearly documented and understood. 
Respond to unexpected site conditions   
• Notify the designer if unexpected groundwater or wet ground conditions are 
encountered during excavation. Seek direction on managing moisture and preventing 
water accumulation beneath or against the building. 
Protect materials on site   
• Keep materials dry before installation by protecting them from: 
• rain and surface water runoff 
• damp rising from the ground 
• condensation dripping from sheltering roofs or temporary coverings (such as 
tarpaulins) placed over materials stored on uncovered ground. 
Avoid enclosure of wet materials  
• Do not install wet materials. Protect installed materials from rain, dew, and wet site 
activities. If materials become wet, allow them to dry fully before enclosure, 
especially for materials that absorb water such as insulation, plasterboard, masonry, 
timber and wood-based products.

Maintain ventilation pathways   
• Confirm that all ventilation pathways specified in the design remain unobstructed 
during construction and are clear at handover. Check that ventilation openings and 
weepholes remain clear especially during installation of insulation. 
Provide ventilation during construction   
• Where safe and appropriate, keep trickle ventilators and other secure openings open 
until handover, particularly when the building is sealed overnight. If internal surface 
condensation is observed, consider additional temporary ventilation or 
dehumidification. 
6.2.3  Communicating  the condensation control strategy at handover  
Explain  the drying  out period  
• Inform building users that moisture introduced during construction may take a year or 
more to dissipate fully. Explain that appropriate ventilation is particularly important 
during early occupation to assist drying and reduce the risk of condensation. 
Explain installed ventilation features   
• Identify and explain the purpose and correct use of: 
• bathroom, laundry, and kitchen exhaust systems 
• any ventilation provided beyond NCC minimum requirements (for example, trickle 
ventilators incorporated in window systems).  
• Emphasise how these features help manage indoor moisture during normal 
occupation. 
Highlight external ventilation openings   
• Identify ventilation openings serving subfloor spaces, wall cavities and roof spaces. 
Explain the need to keep these openings clear and unobstructed, including avoiding 
blockage by landscaping, garden beds, stored items, plants or creepers, as 
obstruction can increase condensation and moisture-related risks.

Vapour permeable membrane : A membrane intended to allow the transmission of water 
vapour. This is often referred to as a breathable membrane or breathable underlay. 
Vapour  resistance : A measure of a material’s reluctance to let water vapour pass through. 
The reciprocal of vapour permeance. 
Verification Method:  A test, inspection, calculation or other method that determines 
whether a Performance Solution complies with the relevant Performance Requirements. 
Water control layer:  A pliable building membrane or the exterior cladding when no 
pliable building membrane is present. 
Waterproof:  The property of a material that does not allow water to penetrate through it.

Appendix C  Compliance with the NCC  
C.1 Responsibilities for regulation of building and 
plumbing in Australia 
State and territory governments are responsible for regulation of building, plumbing and 
development/planning in their respective state or territory. 
The NCC is a joint initiative of the Commonwealth and State and Territory Governments in 
Australia and is produced and maintained by the ABCB on behalf of the Australian 
Government and each state and territory government. The NCC provides a uniform set of 
technical provisions for the design and construction of buildings and other structures, and 
plumbing and drainage systems throughout Australia. It allows for variations in climate and 
geological or geographic conditions. 
The NCC is given legal effect by building and plumbing regulatory legislation in each state 
and territory. This legislation consists of an Act of Parliament and subordinate legislation 
(e.g. building regulations) which empowers the regulation of certain aspects of buildings 
and structures, and contains the administrative provisions necessary to give effect to the 
legislation. 
Each state and territory's legislation adopts the NCC subject to the variation or deletion of 
some of its provisions, or the addition of extra provisions. These variations, deletions and 
additions are generally signposted within the relevant section of the NCC, and located 
within appendices to the NCC. Notwithstanding this, any provision of the NCC may be 
overridden by, or subject to, state or territory legislation. The NCC must therefore be read 
in conjunction with that legislation. 
C.2 Demonstrating compliance with the NCC 
Compliance with the NCC is achieved by complying with the NCC Governing 
Requirements and relevant Performance Requirements. 
The Governing Requirements are a set of governing rules outlining how the NCC must be 
used and the process that must be followed. 
The Performance Requirements prescribe the minimum necessary requirements for 
buildings, building elements, and plumbing and drainage systems. They must be met to 
demonstrate compliance with the NCC. 
There are 3 options available to demonstrate compliance with the Performance 
Requirements. These are:

Appendix D  Standards  
This appendix covers key Australian and international standards relevant to condensation. 
There is no Australian Standard that solely addresses the management of condensation. 
However, standards dealing with roofing, wall cladding, insulation, masonry, and bushfire 
management, all influence how designers and builders manage condensation-related 
issues. 
There are international standards that specifically address condensation. These standards 
are generally developed for European climate conditions and are often based on 
construction practices that are not common in Australia. As a result, designers should 
carefully consider their applicability before relying on international Standards in an 
Australian context. 
 
Alert  
The standards referenced in this section may be updated from time to time. For NCC 
referenced standards, users should refer to the NCC list of referenced documents to 
identify the applicable version. For other standards, the latest edition in force at the time 
of application should be confirmed independently.
